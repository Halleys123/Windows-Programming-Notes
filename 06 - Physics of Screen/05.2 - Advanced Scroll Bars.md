Most developers assume "scrolling is scrolling." But as you've just realized, a modern web browser does not have one scrolling engine. **It has three completely separate physics engines running simultaneously**, depending entirely on which piece of plastic the user's hand is currently touching.

### 1. Scroll Bar Grabbing (The Absolute Lock)

When you click and hold the scroll bar thumb, the browser completely disables all easing, all timers, and all animations.

It establishes a **1:1 Hardware Lock** between the physical mouse sensor and the document's Y-coordinate.

- If your hand moves the mouse 1 pixel down, the document instantly moves exactly _N_ pixels up.    
- If your hand stops moving, the document stops instantly. Zero momentum. Zero glide.
- **The Math:** `relativeY = (mouseY / trackHeight) * totalDocumentHeight`.

If a browser accidentally applied "smooth scrolling" easing to a scroll bar thumb, it would feel like dragging the UI through thick mud. The user demands absolute, instantaneous control here.

### 2. Standard Mouse Wheel (The Kinetic "Kick")

A standard mouse wheel is a **Discrete Input**. It does not send continuous data; it sends chunky, isolated hardware interrupts (the notches).

If the browser moved the text instantly on every notch, it would look jarring and give the user a headache. So, the browser treats the mouse wheel like a kick drum.

- Every "notch" adds a burst of velocity to a target variable.
- An animation timer (like the `WM_TIMER` we built) takes over.
- The timer uses an **Ease-Out Curve**. The text moves very fast initially to show responsiveness, but then decelerates smoothly to a stop over the next ~150 milliseconds.
- If the user spins the wheel rapidly, the browser stacks the "kicks," increasing the target destination and pushing the velocity higher.

### 3. Precision Touchpad (Continuous Momentum Physics)

This is the masterpiece of modern OS engineering. A precision touchpad is a **Continuous Input**. When you drag two fingers, it floods the OS message queue with high-resolution, sub-pixel deltas at 120 times per second.

The browser handles this in two distinct phases:

- **Phase A (Fingers Down):** While your fingers are on the glass, the browser acts exactly like the Scroll Bar Lock. It maps your finger's physical movement 1:1 to the text. It feels like you are physically touching the paper.
- **Phase B (The Release Flick):** The exact millisecond you lift your fingers, the browser calculates your "Release Velocity" (how fast your fingers were moving in the last 50ms). It then hands that velocity over to a **Friction Engine**. The text continues sliding on its own, and the browser mathematically applies a friction coefficient to slow the text down naturally over several seconds until it stops.

If you want your Win32 app to feel like a browser, your `wndProc` must dynamically switch between these three mathematical models depending on whether the message was `WM_MOUSEMOVE` (with capture), `WM_MOUSEWHEEL` (with a delta of 120), or `WM_MOUSEWHEEL` (with a high-resolution sub-120 delta).
